I Started With a Game.
Then I Couldn't Stop.

How a simple browser arcade game became a seven-tool personal dashboard built by a curious guy who had no business calling himself a developer.

Abdulwajid (Jid)
Data Analyst · Media · Junior Developer · Man City Fan · Nigeria

It started with a square. A red square on a black background, chasing a white dot around a canvas. That was the whole thing. No backend, no database, no grand vision. Just a browser game called Square Dodger, built in a weekend, deployed to Netlify, and shared with about three people who didn't ask to see it.

I am, by training, a media person. Radio news writer. The kind of person who writes scripts, not code. But something about that little game, the fact that I had made a thing that existed on the internet, that anyone with a link could open, did something to me that I haven't quite recovered from.

"I had made a thing that existed on the internet. That anyone with a link could open. Something about that hasn't quite left me."

I. The First Expansion

A game was fine. But what if it lived inside something bigger? I had been wanting to build a personal dashboard, a single page that showed me everything I cared about without having to open twelve tabs. Naira exchange rates. Crypto prices. Nigeria weather. Man City fixtures. The usual Nigerian-with-too-many-interests checklist.

So I embedded the game. Into a dashboard. That I then had to build from scratch. In HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript. With no prior experience doing any of this at any kind of scale.

The first version was rough. But it had the bones: live FX rates from an open API. Weather for four Nigerian cities. A Man City card with a fixture and a countdown timer. And the game, sitting there in a tab, completely incongruous with everything else, which somehow made it perfect.

II. The Refinements (and the Breaks)

What happened next was a pattern I would repeat many times: build something, break it completely trying to add one more thing, then fix it and end up with something better than the original.

The first major break came when I tried to add the Nigerian Fintech Landscape 2026 — a full interactive intelligence report on Nigeria's fintech ecosystem — as a tab inside the tools section. I saved the file with the wrong name. Deployed it. And spent an hour wondering why my dashboard had turned into a fintech report with no way back. The fix was embarrassingly simple once I understood it: the report needed to be its own file, not a replacement for the main page.

Then came the Man City data problem. I had wired up a football API — football-data.org — to auto-update the fixture card. Spent a full session building it, proud of the live data. Then discovered that browser-based JavaScript calls to their API are blocked by CORS. The API required server-side calls. I had no server. I had a static site.

"The simple solution was almost always better than the clever one. A JSON file I update in 30 seconds beats an API that doesn't work."

The solution was a single JSON file — city.json — sitting in the same repo as everything else. After every match, I open GitHub, change four values, commit. The dashboard reads it within five minutes. No API, no server, no CORS. Sometimes the simple solution is just better.

III. When It Became Something Real

The dashboard stopped feeling like a project and started feeling like a product somewhere around the fourth or fifth major addition. There was now a 2026 Wealth Path Simulator — compound interest calculations, four fintech strategies, an animated breakdown bar. A Nigerian payment fee calculator comparing Flutterwave, Paystack, Monnify, and Squad side by side. An AI-powered movie recommender that knew about Nollywood.

Then TrueProfit. That one felt different. The idea was simple: most Nigerian small business owners don't know their real profit because they don't count all their costs. Delivery fees. Data bills. Platform charges. Packaging. The things that feel too small to track but compound into serious money quietly leaving your pocket every week.

TrueProfit became the most complete tool on the dashboard with four tabs, twelve pre-loaded hidden expense checkboxes, scenario comparison, a trend chart, PDF export, and smart insights that read your actual numbers and tell you what they mean in plain language. What started as a simple calculator turned into something I genuinely believe could help people make better decisions about their businesses.

IV. The Numbers

By the time the Naira Time Machine went live, a purchasing power visualizer using 21 years of sourced Nigerian economic data, the dashboard had accumulated:

7 tools inside the My Tools section alone. 10,000+ lines of custom HTML, CSS, and JavaScript across all files. 6 Nigerian news sources aggregated in the Daily Digest, updating every 30 minutes. 4 live APIs running simultaneously on every page load — exchange rates, crypto prices, weather, and a cache-busted JSON file for football data. Zero frameworks. No React. No Vue. No libraries except Google Fonts. Everything written by hand.

"Zero frameworks. No React. No Vue. Everything written by hand, line by line, broken and rebuilt and broken again."

V. What This Actually Is

I keep being asked what the dashboard is. The honest answer is that it's a document of learning. Every tool on it represents something I didn't know how to do when I started, figured out by breaking it, reading what broke, and trying again. The Fintech report taught me data visualisation. TrueProfit taught me localStorage and multi-tab UI architecture. The Time Machine taught me Canvas API and how to make hardcoded historical data feel dynamic and alive.

But it's also something I actually use. I check the naira rate on it. I run the wealth simulator when I'm thinking about saving strategies. I opened TrueProfit for a friend who runs a small business in Ilorin and watched her realise, in real time, that she was making about 40% less profit than she thought — because she'd never counted her delivery costs, her data bill, or the Flutterwave fees she paid on every transaction.

That moment, watching a tool I built do the thing it was designed to do for a real person with a real business problem, is the thing I'll remember longer than any line of code.

VI. What Comes Next

The dashboard isn't finished. It probably never will be. That's the thing about building in public, you share the current version knowing it's already behind the next idea you're working on. There are DECODE articles being drafted. A hotel database portfolio project that will eventually become a proper data analysis case study.

But mostly, I want to keep building things that are useful to Nigerians specifically. Not in a generic "African market" way, but in a specific, contextual, ₦50,000 that could buy 7 bags of rice in 2010 but barely buys half a bag today kind of way. The kind of tool that makes someone say: I didn't know it was that bad. Or: wait, I'm actually losing money. Or: okay, now I understand what's happening to my savings.

I started with a game. A red square chasing a white dot. Somewhere between that and the Naira Time Machine, I accidentally became a builder. I don't know exactly when it happened. But I'm not going back.

Profile · April 2026

The Man Who Made Nigeria's Pain
Into a Chart

A young and curious guy named Abdulwajid (JID) built a tool that shows exactly what inflation has stolen from ordinary Nigerians — one bag of rice, one litre of fuel at a time. This is the story behind it.

Written about Abdulwajid (Jid)
TECHVIEW Intelligence · April 2026 · 5 min read

Somewhere in Nigeria, a young and curious guy is sitting in front of a laptop that has survived a power cut, and he is thinking about rice. Not cooking it. Pricing it. He remembers when you could walk into a market in 2015 and come out with a 50-kilogram bag for about eight thousand naira. He remembers when that changed. He remembers exactly when it stopped feeling gradual and started feeling like falling.

Abdulwajid, known online as Jid, works at a news desk. He writes and produces broadcast scripts daily, covering everything from federal policy to local events. He is also, quietly, a data analyst and self-taught developer who two years ago opened a code editor for the first time and has not really closed it since. The thing he has been building most recently is not a game or a dashboard widget. It is a reckoning.

"People know things are expensive. But they don't know how expensive. There's a difference between feeling the pain and seeing the number."

— Abdulwajid, on why he built the Naira Time Machine
I. The Price of Things

Ask any Nigerian above the age of twenty-five what petrol used to cost and they will tell you without hesitation. Sixty-five naira a litre. That number lived in the national memory like a scar - fixed, almost mythological, the last price that felt stable before the country's relationship with its own currency began its long deterioration. Then in 2016 it became one hundred and forty-five naira. In 2023, on the day a new president declared the fuel subsidy gone, it became six hundred and seventeen. By 2026 it had settled above one thousand three hundred naira. One litre of petrol. The same litre.

Abdulwajid watched this happen the way most Nigerians did, at the pump, at the market, in the gap between what his salary could buy last year and what it buys now. But unlike most people, he decided to measure it. Not in complaints. In data.

"People know things are expensive," he says. "But they don't know how expensive. There is a difference between feeling the pain and seeing the number. When you type in fifty thousand naira and see that it bought 7.7 bags of rice in 2010 but barely half a bag today, that is not a feeling anymore. That is a fact."

II. Building the Evidence

The Naira Time Machine, the tool he built and embedded inside his personal dashboard, is deceptively simple on the surface. You enter an amount. You select a year, anywhere from 2005 to 2025. The tool shows you what that money could buy then versus what it buys today in 2026, across four categories: bags of rice, litres of petrol, US dollars, and days of minimum wage work. Animated bars compare the two eras side by side. A chart traces Nigeria's annual inflation rate across two decades. A section of historical context explains what policy decisions drove each period of price movement.

Behind that simplicity is 21 years of sourced economic data, exchange rates from CBN historical data, inflation figures from the World Bank and IMF. Every number is cited. None of it is invented. Abdulwajid, whose media training makes him allergic to unsourced claims, spent considerable time verifying each data point before a single line of code was written.

"This is not an opinion piece. The numbers are the argument. I just made them visible."

— Abdulwajid

"This is not an opinion piece," he says, with the measured firmness of someone who has spent years writing for the media. "The numbers are the argument. I just made them visible."

III. The Year That Changed Everything

The data has a villain, and it is 2023. When President Bola Tinubu removed the fuel subsidy on his inauguration day, declaring "subsidy is gone" in a sentence that would define the economic mood of the next two years, the consequences cascaded through every market in Nigeria within weeks. Petrol tripled. The naira, already under pressure, was then floated by the Central Bank and lost seventy percent of its value against the dollar in months. A bag of rice that cost sixty-five thousand naira at the start of 2023 cost ninety-five thousand by 2024. The minimum wage could no longer buy a single full bag of the country's staple food.

Abdulwajid's tool makes this visible in a way that a headline cannot. Type in thirty thousand naira and select 2019 — the year that wage was set — and the tool shows you that it could buy 1.2 bags of rice. Select 2024 with the same amount and it shows you 0.3 bags. The minimum wage, in rice terms, lost seventy-five percent of its value in five years. That is not a statistic. That is a meal that did not happen.

IV. Why a Journalist Built This

Abdulwajid is not an economist. He is a self-taught developer who started writing code two years ago with no prior training and no roadmap. What he has and what shows in this tool more than anywhere else, is his instinct for what makes data mean something to a person who is not looking for data.

He built a side DECODE brand around a single idea: making complex things accessible. Philosophy, psychology, economics, data - stripped of jargon, delivered with clarity. The Naira Time Machine is the most literal expression of that idea he has produced. It takes macroeconomic data that lives in NBS spreadsheets and World Bank reports and turns it into something a market trader in Kano can look at and say: so that's what happened to my money.

That sentence - so that's what happened to my money - is the whole point. Not a policy paper. Not a thread. A tool that makes the abstract personal, the statistical human, and the invisible finally, undeniably visible.

Jumoke Didn't Know
She Was Losing Money.

A small business owner in Lagos thought she was profitable. A developer named Jid built a tool that showed her she wasn't and in doing so, built something that could help millions of Nigerian entrepreneurs see what they've been missing.

Written about Abdulwajid (Jid)
TECHVIEW Intelligence · May 2026 · 6 min read

Jumoke runs a small food delivery and perfume business in the business capital of Nigeria - Lagos. She is organised, consistent, and by any reasonable measure, busy. Orders come in. Deliveries go out. Money moves. She had been at it long enough to feel like she understood her numbers, she knew what she charged, she knew what stock cost, and at the end of most weeks there was something left over. She thought she was profitable. She was wrong.

The moment she discovered this did not happen in an accountant's office or a business advisory session. It happened on a laptop screen, using a free tool built by a self-taught developer named Abdulwajid, who had created it partly because he suspected Jumoke's situation was not unique and that across Nigeria, thousands of small business owners were quietly losing money they did not know they were losing.

"She had never counted her delivery costs. Her data bill. The Flutterwave fees on every transaction. When we added them up, her actual margin was about forty percent lower than she thought."

— Abdulwajid, on the moment TrueProfit proved its purpose
I. The Costs Nobody Counts

In Nigeria's informal economy, which is to say, in most of Nigeria's actual economy, profit is typically calculated the simple way. Money in, minus the cost of goods, equals what you made. It is a method that feels logical and is almost always wrong. It ignores the fuel to make the delivery. It ignores the data bill used to respond to customers on WhatsApp at midnight. It ignores the packaging, the platform fees, the bank charges on every transfer, the returns, the equipment that quietly wore out last month. Individually, each of these costs feels too small to track. Collectively, they can consume a third of your actual revenue without you ever noticing.

Abdulwajid, who writes news scripts for an International broadcast media by day and builds data tools by night, had been thinking about this problem for a while before he built anything. He had watched people around him run businesses with genuine effort and real customers, and still struggle. "The hustle was not the issue," he says. "The accounting was. And not complicated accounting, just the basic act of counting everything, not just the obvious things."

II. What TrueProfit Actually Does

TrueProfit, the tool he built and embedded in his personal dashboard, starts with a straightforward premise: most profit calculators only ask you about revenue and stock cost. TrueProfit asks about everything else too. Twelve categories of hidden expenses - delivery and transport, packaging, data and airtime, platform and gateway fees, marketing, utilities, rent, equipment, returns, storage, insurance, bank charges, are presented as checkboxes. Tick the ones that apply to your business, enter the amount, and the tool folds them into the calculation. There is also an open miscellaneous field for anything not on the list.

The output is not just a profit figure. It is a full breakdown of net margin, gross margin, return on investment, profit per unit sold, and a visual bar showing exactly where every naira of revenue went. A smart insights engine reads the numbers and generates specific observations: if hidden costs exceed thirty-five percent of revenue, the tool flags it as dangerous. If the margin is below ten percent, it warns of underpricing. If the margin is above thirty percent, it tells you so and explains why that matters for scaling.

"I did not want it to just show you a number," Abdulwajid explains. "I wanted it to tell you what the number means. Because most people can see a number and still not know what to do with it."

III. The Moment It Worked

When Jumoke sat down with the tool for the first time, she entered her revenue and her stock costs - the numbers she already knew. Then she started working through the checkboxes. Delivery fees: yes. Data and airtime: yes. Flutterwave charges on every order payment: yes. Packaging for the food orders: yes. Each tick revealed a cost she had never formally counted. When the calculation ran, her projected profit margin was roughly forty percent lower than what she had assumed going in.

"She went quiet for a moment," Abdulwajid recalls. "Then she said, so I have been working for less than I thought this whole time. And I said, yes. But now you know. And knowing is the starting point."

That exchange, quiet, unglamorous, consequential is the reason TrueProfit exists. Not a pitch deck moment. Not a viral demo. Just a person in Lagos seeing their business clearly, perhaps for the first time, because the right questions had finally been asked in the right order.

"Now you know. And knowing is the starting point."

— Abdulwajid, to Jumoke
IV. Built for the Market Trader, Not the MBA

What distinguishes TrueProfit from the business calculators that already exist is not sophistication, it is specificity. The hidden expense categories are not generic. They are drawn from the texture of Nigerian small business life: the Flutterwave fee on every transaction, the data bill that never appears in any accounting spreadsheet, the cost of fuel for the delivery that felt too small to write down. The tool speaks the language of the person running a food delivery operation from their phone, not the language of someone with a finance degree.

It supports seven currencies: naira, dollar, pound, euro, and three others which means it is equally useful for a Nigerian business owner in Lagos, a Kenyan entrepreneur in Nairobi, or a member of the diaspora running a side hustle from London. But its soul is Nigerian. Its examples are Nigerian. Its intuition about what costs get forgotten comes from watching Nigerian businesses operate up close.

Abdulwajid is a media person by training and a developer by stubbornness. He has been writing code for two years. He has no formal computer science background. What he has is an eye for the story that data tells and the patience to build the thing that makes that story visible. TrueProfit is that thing. Jumoke's business is more honest now because of it. So is the conversation about what it actually means to run something in Nigeria in 2026.
